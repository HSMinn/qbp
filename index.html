Quantum-Boltzmann-Protein

Sungkyunkwan UniversityInterdisciplinary Research Project

An interdisciplinary research project at Sungkyunkwan University. We test whether quantum-assisted methods are actually better than strong classical methods at sampling the energy landscapes of proteins and biomolecules.

Premises

The premises behind this research

We design the research on four premises.

Model

Described by energy

We assign an energy to each state of a biomolecule and define a Boltzmann-form target distribution from it. Whether the model's energy and temperature correspond to physical energy and temperature has to be checked separately.

P(s) = e−E(s)/kBT / Z

Z is a normalizing constant. The meaning of E and T is set model by model.

Sampling

We work on problems where sampling is hard

When many wells are separated by energy barriers, a sampler can miss some wells or mis-estimate the probability mass each well should receive.

Hypothesis

Quantum is a hypothesis to test

Whether quantum-assisted sampling reduces this bias is a hypothesis to test. We make no claim of a shorter running time.

Comparison

We compare fairly

We compare only against strong classical baselines under the same target distribution, the same temperature, and the same resource budget, and we fix the criteria for judgment before seeing results.

Aim

If strong classical methods miss the probability mass of some wells, we determine whether quantum-assisted sampling recovers it more accurately.

If classical methods alone remove the bias, the quantum path stops there. If they do not, we also ask whether the improvement survives a fair comparison that includes hardware calibration and resource costs, and whether it holds up at experimental endpoints.

We split the work into four stages and test one alternative explanation at each stage. Depending on the result, we proceed, switch course, or stop.

  1. Energy identification
  2. Classical sampling
  3. Quantum comparison
  4. Experimental validation

Small Project

A small project within the Aim

Rather than take on the whole Aim at once, we start with one small, well-scoped case.

Small Project

Re-ranking EphB1 candidates by function

Question
With the same candidate pool and the same review budget, does the statistical coupling information of residue pairs outside direct structural neighbors, estimated from natural sequences, help select higher-function candidates better?
Scope
A short-term case study currently under way within the Aim. It does not replace the groundwork for the Aim as a whole.
Status
We have completed a technical pilot of two models that use structural information. Whether the coupling information actually improves function-based selection, which is the answer to the question above, has not been determined yet.
